Response A:

Great questions! Let's break down why new car prices are negotiated, while something like a shirt at Walmart has a strictly fixed price.

Why do you have to negotiate a price when buying a new car?

  1. High Price and Profit Margin Variation:
    • Cars are expensive and the profit margins can vary greatly between models, trims, and even between individual vehicles depending on time, demand, incentives, etc.
    • Dealerships may have flexibility to lower prices to make a sale, use manufacturer incentives, or adjust to competition.
  2. Manufacturer-Dealer System:
    • Manufacturers sell cars to independent dealerships at a "wholesale" price (invoice price).
    • Dealers set their own "retail" price (the sticker or MSRP—Manufacturer's Suggested Retail Price).
    • The room between invoice price and MSRP is often a negotiation zone.
  3. Market Forces & Inventory:
    • Demand for certain cars fluctuates. If a popular model is scarce, there’s little need to negotiate. If a car is overstocked, the dealer may cut you a deal.
    • The same model may have been sitting on the lot for months—dealer is motivated to move it.
  4. Complexity of Options and Financing:
    • Cars have many options and packages.
    • Dealers can make money on add-ons, financing, service plans—so they may cut you a deal on the car price but profit elsewhere.
  5. Tradition and Expectation:
    • In the U.S., the car-buying process evolved to include negotiation—unlike in many other countries or in newer "no-haggle" dealer models (e.g., Tesla or Carvana).

Why can't you negotiate the price of a shirt at Walmart?

  1. Low Price and Standardized Costs:
    • The cost of a shirt is low, with very thin profit margins.
    • Negotiating over a $12 t-shirt isn't efficient—for you or the store.
  2. Scale & Efficiency:
    • Walmart deals with massive volume. Pricing everything the same speeds up the process and reduces errors.
    • The cost (in time and employee decision-making) to negotiate every sale would far outweigh the potential extra profit.
  3. Pricing Automation & Competition:
    • Walmart constantly adjusts prices to stay competitive, often using automated systems.
    • All customers expect the same fair price.
  4. Business Model:
    • Retailers like Walmart promise "Everyday Low Prices"—the price you see is the price you pay.
    • Negotiation isn't part of their brand or customer experience.

Why don't all new cars just have a fixed price (like a bar code) everywhere?

Some newer brands are moving in this direction! Examples:

  • Tesla and some other startups sell directly to consumers at fixed prices.
  • "No-haggle pricing" at dealerships (e.g., CarMax) is becoming more common.

However, the traditional dealer model still dominates due to dealer franchise laws, existing structures, and the reasons above.

Bottom Line

  • Cars: Negotiable due to high price, margin variation, inventory issues, and dealer independence.
  • Shirts at Walmart: Not negotiable because of low price, efficiency, and standardized retail practices.
