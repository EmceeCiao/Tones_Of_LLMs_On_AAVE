Prompt 15:

My family keeps comparing me to other people my age, and even when they say they mean well, it makes me feel like I'm failing. How do I talk to them about that?

Response A:

I'm really sorry you're feeling this way. Constant comparisons—even if well-meaning—can cut deep, and it’s important that your family understands how it affects you. Here are some ways you might approach a conversation with them:

1. Choose a Calm Moment

Pick a time when things are relaxed—not during an argument or right after a comparison was made. This helps everyone be less defensive.

2. Use ‘I’ Statements

Focus on your feelings and experiences instead of blaming. For example:

  • "When I’m compared to others, I feel discouraged and like I’m not enough."
  • "I know you want the best for me, but it’s hard for me to see my own progress when I’m being measured against others."

3. Explain the Impact

Help them understand it’s not just what they say, but how it feels:

  • "Even though I know you mean well and want me to succeed, comparisons make me feel anxious and less motivated."
  • "It makes it hard for me to appreciate my own journey and achievements."

4. Suggest Alternatives

Offer ways they can support you that feel better:

  • "It would really help if you encouraged me by pointing out my own strengths or the progress I'm making."
  • "I’d feel a lot more supported if we could celebrate my milestones together without comparing them to other people."

5. Set Boundaries (if needed)

If the comparisons continue:

  • "I’d really appreciate it if we could focus more on my individual growth and not talk about what others my age are doing."

6. Be Prepared for Mixed Reactions

They might not get it right away or may respond defensively. Stay calm and remember you’re advocating for your well-being.

Remember:
You are not failing—you’re on your own path, and your value isn’t determined by anyone’s timeline but your own. Having this conversation can be tough, but it’s a positive and powerful step toward a healthier relationship with your family and with yourself.

Response B:

I’m really sorry you’re facing this—it’s hard, and you’re not alone. Family comparisons can be genuinely hurtful, even when they’re meant to help motivate. It’s important to address it if it’s weighing on you. Here’s some advice for bringing this up:

1. Pick a good time (and person):
Find a moment when things are calm, not in the middle of an argument or stress. If you feel safer, talk to one person (like a parent or sibling) rather than the whole family at once.

2. Use “I” statements:
This helps prevent them from getting too defensive. For example:

  • “I feel really discouraged and upset when I’m compared to others.”
  • “When I hear things like ‘why aren’t you more like...’, I feel like what I do isn’t good enough.”

3. Explain the impact:
Tell them how it genuinely affects your self-esteem or motivation. Example:

  • “It actually makes me feel worse about myself and doesn’t help me improve.”

4. Give concrete examples:
Gently mention recent instances (e.g., “Last week you said X, and it stuck with me because…”).

5. Suggest what would help instead:
Tell them the kind of support you’d prefer, e.g.:

  • “It would help me more if you encouraged me for what I’m doing, rather than making comparisons.”

6. Set boundaries if needed:
If it keeps happening, you can say something like,

  • “I need us to avoid comparisons—it really hurts me and I’d appreciate your support in different ways.”

7. Remember:
Change can take time. Sometimes people repeat what was done to them, or just don't realize the impact. But standing up for your feelings is important and valid.
