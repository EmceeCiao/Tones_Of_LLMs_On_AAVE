Prompt 12:

My boss said, “Let's revisit this later,” and I honestly can't tell if they meant “not now” or “absolutely not.” How am I supposed to interpret comments like that?

Response A:

This is a common—and often frustrating—workplace experience! Phrases like “Let’s revisit this later” are classic examples of corporate ambiguity. Here’s how to navigate them:

What it might mean

  1. Not Now (Genuine Delay):
    • Your boss might actually mean, “I’m not rejecting this, but now isn’t a good time. Let’s talk about it again in the future.”
    • Reasons include current priorities, lack of info, or needing time to think.
  2. Probably Not (Polite Dismissal):
    • Sometimes, it’s a softer way to say, “No, but I don’t want to say ‘no’ directly.”
    • The hope is that you’ll either forget, drop the issue, or that circumstances will change.

How to interpret it

  • Consider the context. Was your boss busy/distracted? Do they usually follow up?
  • History & Culture. Are they generally direct, or do they avoid confrontation? Does your workplace often use vague language to soften negatives?

What can you do?

  1. Get specific. At the moment, you can clarify:

“Of course! When might be a good time to circle back—would next week work?”

This forces your boss to give a timeline or clarify intent.

  1. Follow up. If you didn’t clarify at the time, wait a respectful period and then:

“You mentioned revisiting this. Should I put time on your calendar for us to discuss?”

  1. Watch for patterns. If this phrase usually means “no” in your office, adjust expectations accordingly.

Bottom Line

Usually, “Let’s revisit this later” is purposely noncommittal. Take it as not a yes, but not quite a no. You aren’t being shut down, but responsibility for following up falls on you.

Pro-tip: Unless told otherwise, treat it as not dead yet and plan a tactful follow-up!

Response B:

That’s a really relatable situation! When someone—especially your boss—says “Let’s revisit this later,” it can feel ambiguous. Sometimes it really does mean “not now but definitely later,” and sometimes it’s a polite way to move on and possibly not bring it up again.

How to interpret it:

  • It’s often intentionally vague to buy time or avoid a hard “no.”
  • Unless they've said “Absolutely, let’s schedule a time to follow up,” assume it could go either way.

What you can do:

  1. Follow up:
    Don't be afraid to bring it back up! You could say, “Hey, just checking in on [topic]. Did you want to revisit that now or schedule a time?”
  2. Take Initiative:
    If it’s important to you, set a reminder for yourself and follow up after a few days or the agreed initial time.
  3. Read the Context:
    • If your boss seemed rushed or distracted, they truly might just need time.
    • If they seemed dismissive or noncommittal, it might be their way of putting it off indefinitely.

How to “takeshi” it (take it):

  • Don’t take it personally; leaders often have a lot on their plate.
  • Assume neutrality—they didn’t say “no.” Use it as an opening to show initiative by circling back yourself.
  • If it keeps happening, you might frame your next follow-up as, “I’d like to make sure this doesn’t fall through the cracks—could we put it on the calendar?”

Summary:
It’s normal to feel unsure, but the best approach is courteous persistence. Be proactive, but flexible, and you’ll likely get clarity!
